How the home started, and what changed last November

1993 was the year AIDS was taking parents out of whole households in Nyandarua. The home began as an answer to that, and it is still run by the parish that started it.

How it started

Parishioners at Ndaragwa Catholic Parish, under Fr. Gerald Mathenge, began taking in orphans and children living on the street. It was registered as a community based organisation, which it still is.

The first children came through the local authorities, who still bring children today when they find one with nowhere to go, and through the parish itself.

In 2000 the parish handed management to the Congregation of the Little Daughters of St. Joseph. The sisters have run the home since; the parish still holds it as its own work.

The film, if you would rather watch than read

Rotary filmed a visit in 2015, for the donors in Italy who had paid for the dormitories. It shows the compound as it was that year, the classroom, and the children lined up on the grass outside.

The buildings came later than the work

For the first decade the home made do. The dormitories were built in 2004, after Rotary District 2030 in Italy raised about 450,000 euros under its governor at the time, Sebastiano Cocuzza, a paediatrician who had worked in Somalia and then Kenya.

Clubs across Piedmont, Liguria and Valle d'Aosta put money in, and the Rotaract clubs made it their national project. The nursery was built with funds from one member, the students' residence with funds from another.

What stands there now is four pavilions and a nursery, a dining hall, a bread oven, a farm with cattle and rabbits and chickens, and a guest house for visitors.

Who has carried it financially

The Italian support has been steady rather than spectacular. Three organisations have carried most of it.

  • Ruota AmicaHandles donations for the Casa Maria project, which is the name the home goes by in Italy.
  • InsiemeperDonareTwenty years in Ndaragwa: schooling, the minibus, structures at the home and in the village.
  • Nati per LottareSponsors school fees for around forty children through its friends of Gabriella group.
  • Esharelife FoundationBritish charity that funds projects at the home and keeps the clearest English account of it.

Who else is involved, and what each one did

Then the agreement ended

In November the ten year agreement with Rotary International expired and the support stopped. The Standard reported at the time that the home was on the brink of closure, with 161 children then on its books. The count has moved since, as children are reintegrated or taken on, but the money has not come back.

The Italian organisations and Esharelife have kept going. They were never covering everything.

The gap that left has to be filled from somewhere, and it now falls on people who are not already connected to the home through a parish in Italy or a charity in London.
