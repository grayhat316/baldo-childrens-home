Home / Life at the home

Four pavilions, a farm, a kitchen that needs replacing, and water twice a week

What a normal week looks like at the home, from the dormitories to the shamba that pays the electricity bill.

The compound

Four pavilions

Two for girls, two for boys, with hot and cold water in the rooms. The nursery holds the smallest children.

The kitchen

Feeds around a hundred children three times a day. It is on the home's own list of things that need replacing.

The farm

Maize, wheat, potatoes, onions, cabbages, sukuma wiki and spinach. Cattle for milk, chickens for eggs, rabbits and pigs for meat.

The guest house

For visitors and volunteers, some staying a day and some a month.

The farm pays the bills

Most of what the land produces is eaten at the home. The surplus goes to the market in Ndaragwa, and the money from it covers the water and the electricity.

It is not a large income. It is the reason the bills still get paid in a bad month.

Children take part in the lighter work: grazing the cows, feeding the rabbits and chickens. Older ones get career guidance in the holidays instead.

Water is the constraint behind everything

Ndaragwa is dry. The supply reaches the compound about twice a week. Cooking, washing, the animals and the vegetables all run off what is held between deliveries.

It is why a water tank is the sort of gift that changes a week rather than a day.

The home's own paperwork lists three standing needs: a new kitchen, school uniforms, and fees to keep children in secondary school.

A day

School takes the morning. Children at the state schools nearby walk or take the minibus; the nursery stays on the compound. Lunch is served in the dining hall, or outside when the weather allows.

After school there is the farm, homework and games. In the holidays there is career guidance for the older ones, sport against other children's homes, and a talent day.
