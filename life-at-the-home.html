Life at the home

Four pavilions, a nursery, a farm, a kitchen that needs replacing, and water that arrives twice a week.

The compound

The children sleep in four pavilions, two for girls and two for boys, with the nursery for the smallest. There is hot and cold water in the rooms. Clothes are sewn and repaired on site rather than bought.

The guest house takes the visitors and volunteers who come through, some for a day and some for longer.

The farm

Maize, wheat, potatoes, onions, cabbages, sukuma wiki and spinach grow on the home's land. Cattle give milk, chickens give eggs, rabbits and pigs are raised for meat. Children take part in the lighter work: grazing the cows, feeding the rabbits and chickens.

Most of what the farm produces is eaten at the home. The surplus goes to the market in Ndaragwa, and that money pays the water and the electricity. It is not a large income, but it is the reason the bills get paid in a bad month.

Water

Ndaragwa is dry. The supply reaches the compound about twice a week. Everything that needs water, cooking, washing, the animals, the vegetables, runs off what is held between those deliveries.

It is the constraint that sits behind most of the others, and the reason a tank is the sort of gift that changes a week rather than a day.

The home's own paperwork lists three standing needs: a new kitchen, school uniforms, and fees to keep children in secondary school. If you are giving to something specific, those are the three.

The day

School takes the morning. The children at the state schools nearby walk or take the minibus, and the nursery stays on the compound. Lunch is served outside when the weather allows, from the kitchen.

After school there is the farm, homework, and games. In the school holidays there is career guidance for the older ones, sport against other children's homes, and a talent day.
