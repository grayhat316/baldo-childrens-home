Education

The home pays for school from nursery to university. It also pays for the courses that are not university, which is where a good number of these children end up.

The ladder

Nursery is on the compound, for children between three and five. Primary and secondary are at the state schools nearby. After that it depends on the child: college, university, or a vocational course.

Fees are only part of it. A child at a day school needs a uniform, books, and something to carry them in. A child at a boarding school needs the term's supplies. When the home is short of money this is the first place it shows, which is why uniforms appear on the list of needs.

Trades, not only degrees

Carpentry, embroidery and masonry are taught to the young people who are not heading for a degree. It is the practical answer to the question of what happens when a child turns eighteen and the home is no longer their address.

The women and youth outreach programme teaches skills that work the same way for people in the village: soap making, basket weaving, khaki bags, beadwork, shaggy mats. Those run alongside the children's programme, not instead of it.

After school

Rotary funded scholarships for school leavers going on to advanced training. Fourteen young people have used them. The home also runs career guidance in the April, August and December holidays for children from upper primary upwards: which subjects to take, what the paths actually are, what the work looks like.

For the ones looking for work there is help with a CV and a cover letter, and the home takes on young people for attachment and volunteer placements where it can.

Keeping a child in secondary school

Fees, uniform, books and a term's supplies. This is the specific thing the home says it is short of.
